  • Their big question: who am I really, and what is behind everything?
  • Their answer: your true Self (Atman) and the ultimate reality (Brahman) are one: "Tat tvam asi," you are That.

Vedanta

The eleven principal Upanishads

The Upanishads come at the end of each Veda, so they are called Vedanta, "the end of the Veda." More than 200 texts carry the name; tradition lists 108. The 11 below are the oldest and most important, the ones Adi Shankaracharya and later teachers wrote commentaries on. Together with the Bhagavad Gita and the Brahma Sutras, they form the prasthana traya, the three foundations of Vedanta philosophy.

Many of them teach through stories and conversations: a boy and Death, a father and son, a husband and wife, a teacher and six students. Each section below gives the story in simple words, followed by key verses.

1

Isha Upanishad

ईशावास्योपनिषद्

Shukla Yajurveda · 18 verses

The shortest principal Upanishad and the last chapter of the Shukla Yajurveda. In 18 verses it teaches how to live in the world with God in view: see the divine in everything, enjoy with detachment, keep working, and balance practical knowledge with spiritual knowledge. Its first verse is on the main page and its second on the Yajurveda page.

Neither knowledge nor action alone

Isha Upanishad 9

अन्धं तमः प्रविशन्ति येऽविद्यामुपासते ।
ततो भूय इव ते तमो य उ विद्यायां रताः ॥

andhaṃ tamaḥ praviśanti ye 'vidyām upāsate | tato bhūya iva te tamo ya u vidyāyāṃ ratāḥ ||

Word by word
अन्धं तमः
blind darkness
प्रविशन्ति
enter
ये अविद्याम् उपासते
those who follow only worldly knowledge (or ritual)
ततः भूयः इव
into even greater
ते तमः
they (enter) darkness
ये उ विद्यायां रताः
those who delight only in knowledge
Translation

Those who follow only worldly action (avidya) enter blind darkness. Into even greater darkness go those who delight only in knowledge (vidya).

In simple words

A surprising verse: even spiritual knowledge, if cut off from life and duty, becomes a trap. The verses that follow say you need both: worldly skill to live well, and spiritual wisdom to go beyond death.

📖 A story for this verse

A traditional folk tale

A forest caught fire. Two travellers were trapped in it: one was blind, the other could not walk.

The blind man could walk, but he could not see the way out. The lame man could see the way out, but he could not walk. Each alone would have died.

So the blind man lifted the lame man onto his shoulders. "Left! Now straight! Careful, a fallen tree!" called the one on top, and the one below carried them both. Together they came out of the fire safely.

The Isha Upanishad says the same about knowledge and action. Work without understanding is blind; understanding without work is lame. You need both, carrying each other.

2

Kena Upanishad

केनोपनिषद्

Samaveda · 4 sections

"By whom?" Who makes the mind think, the eyes see and the tongue speak? The answer is the Self, which can't be seen because it is the seer. The Upanishad then tells a story: the gods won a battle and became proud. A mysterious spirit (yaksha) appeared. Agni could not burn a single blade of grass it placed before him; Vayu could not blow it away. Then the goddess Uma Haimavati appeared and told Indra that the yaksha was Brahman, by whose power alone the gods had won. Its first verse is on the Samaveda page.

3

Katha Upanishad

कठोपनिषद्

Krishna Yajurveda · 2 chapters, 6 sections

The story of Nachiketa, a boy who meets Death. His father was giving away old, useless cows as charity. Nachiketa asked, "To whom will you give me?" again and again, until his angry father shouted, "To Death!" The boy went to Yama's house and waited three days without food. To make amends, Yama offered three wishes. Nachiketa asked for his father's peace, for the knowledge of the sacred fire, and finally: what happens after death? Yama tried to tempt him with wealth, long life and kingdoms instead. Nachiketa refused them all, so Yama taught him the knowledge of the deathless Self.

The good and the pleasant

Katha Upanishad 1.2.2

श्रेयश्च प्रेयश्च मनुष्यमेतस्तौ सम्परीत्य विविनक्ति धीरः ।
श्रेयो हि धीरोऽभि प्रेयसो वृणीते प्रेयो मन्दो योगक्षेमाद्वृणीते ॥

śreyaś ca preyaś ca manuṣyam etas tau samparītya vivinakti dhīraḥ | śreyo hi dhīro 'bhi preyaso vṛṇīte preyo mando yogakṣemād vṛṇīte ||

Word by word
श्रेयः
the good, the beneficial
प्रेयः
the pleasant, the attractive
धीरः
the wise, steady person
विविनक्ति
tells apart, discriminates
वृणीते
chooses
मन्दः
the dull, foolish
योगक्षेमात्
for gain and keeping
Translation

Both the good (shreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) come to every person. The wise examine them and tell them apart. The wise choose the good over the pleasant; the foolish choose the pleasant, out of greed and attachment.

In simple words

Every day we choose between what feels good now and what is good for us: phone or study, junk food or healthy food, anger or patience. Nachiketa chose the good over every pleasure, and that made him worthy of the highest knowledge.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Valmiki Ramayana (Ayodhya Kanda)

When Bharata returned to Ayodhya, he found his father dead and Rama sent to the forest. His mother Kaikeyi told him proudly, "The kingdom is yours now. Rule it."

It was the pleasant path laid out in front of him: a throne, wealth and power, with no effort at all. Bharata refused it. He scolded his mother, and walked with the whole city to the forest to beg Rama to come back.

When Rama would not break their father's word, Bharata asked for his sandals. For fourteen years he placed them on the throne and ruled only as Rama's servant, living simply outside the city like a hermit.

The Katha Upanishad says the good and the pleasant both come to everyone. The wise choose the good. Bharata did.

The chariot of life

Katha Upanishad 1.3.3–4

आत्मानं रथिनं विद्धि शरीरं रथमेव तु ।
बुद्धिं तु सारथिं विद्धि मनः प्रग्रहमेव च ॥
इन्द्रियाणि हयानाहुर्विषयांस्तेषु गोचरान् ।

ātmānaṃ rathinaṃ viddhi śarīraṃ ratham eva tu | buddhiṃ tu sārathiṃ viddhi manaḥ pragraham eva ca || indriyāṇi hayān āhur viṣayāṃs teṣu gocarān |

Word by word
आत्मानं
the Self
रथिनं विद्धि
know as the owner of the chariot
शरीरं
the body
रथम् एव तु
as the chariot itself
बुद्धिं
the intellect
सारथिं
the charioteer
मनः
the mind
प्रग्रहम्
the reins
इन्द्रियाणि
the senses
हयान् आहुः
they call the horses
विषयान्
the sense objects
तेषु गोचरान्
the roads they travel on
Translation

Know the Self as the owner of the chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know the intellect as the driver, and the mind as the reins. The senses are the horses, and the objects of the senses are the roads they run on.

In simple words

This is one of the best-known pictures in Indian thought. A clear intellect holding a steady mind keeps the senses on the right road, and the journey reaches its goal. See the diagram below.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Mahabharata (Shalya Parva)

For eighteen days of war, Krishna drove Arjuna's chariot. Arjuna held the bow; Krishna held the reins of the white horses.

On the last day, Krishna stopped the chariot and said, "Arjuna, get down first, with your bow." Arjuna was puzzled, since the charioteer usually steps down first, but he obeyed. Then Krishna stepped down. At that moment the chariot burst into flames and burned to ashes.

Krishna explained: "This chariot was struck by the most terrible weapons of Drona and Karna. It was only my holding it that kept it together."

The Katha Upanishad describes life as a chariot: the body is the chariot, the senses are the horses, the mind is the reins, and the intellect is the driver. When the wise driver holds the reins, even a battered chariot reaches its goal.

4

Prashna Upanishad

प्रश्नोपनिषद्

Atharvaveda · 6 questions

Six seekers come to the sage Pippalada with questions. He tells them first to live with him for a year in discipline, and only then ask. The six questions: Where do living beings come from? Which powers support the body? Where does prana (life-breath) come from? What sleeps and what stays awake in us? What does meditation on Om lead to? And where is the Person with sixteen parts? It teaches that a student must be ready before deep answers can help.

5

Mundaka Upanishad

मुण्डकोपनिषद्

Atharvaveda · 3 chapters

A wealthy householder, Shaunaka, asks the sage Angiras: "What is that which, when known, everything becomes known?" Angiras explains that there are two kinds of knowledge. The lower kind includes even the four Vedas and the sciences; the higher is that by which the imperishable is known. This Upanishad gives India its motto, "Satyameva Jayate," and repeats the Rigveda's verse of the two birds on one tree.

Two kinds of knowledge

Mundaka Upanishad 1.1.4

द्वे विद्ये वेदितव्ये इति ह स्म यद्ब्रह्मविदो वदन्ति ।
परा चैवापरा च ॥

dve vidye veditavye iti ha sma yad brahmavido vadanti | parā caivāparā ca ||

Word by word
द्वे विद्ये
two kinds of knowledge
वेदितव्ये
are to be known
इति ह स्म ... वदन्ति
so they say
यत् ब्रह्मविदः
the knowers of Brahman
परा
the higher
च एव अपरा च
and the lower
Translation

Two kinds of knowledge must be known, say the knowers of Brahman: the higher and the lower.

In simple words

Lower knowledge is everything we learn about the world: languages, science, even scripture as information. Higher knowledge is direct realisation of the Self. Both matter, but only the second ends sorrow.

📖 A story for this verse

Told in the Mundaka Upanishad itself (1.1)

Shaunaka was a great householder who ran a large school and had read many books. Still, one question would not leave him.

He went to the sage Angiras with fuel in his hands, the traditional sign of a humble student, and asked: "Sir, what is that which, when it is known, everything else becomes known?"

Angiras smiled. "There are two kinds of knowledge. The lower kind is all the subjects we study: the Vedas themselves, grammar, astronomy, the rules of ritual. The higher kind is the knowledge by which we reach the imperishable, the source of everything."

Shaunaka knew many subjects. He had come for the one thing that ties them all together. Angiras then taught him, and that teaching is the Mundaka Upanishad.

6

Mandukya Upanishad

माण्डूक्योपनिषद्

Atharvaveda · 12 verses

Only 12 verses long, yet the Muktika Upanishad says that this one alone is enough for liberation. It explains the syllable Om as four parts matching the four states of consciousness: waking, dreaming, deep sleep, and turiya, the pure awareness behind them all. Its verse and a diagram are on the Atharvaveda page. Its second verse contains the Mahavakya ayam ātmā brahma, "this Self is Brahman."

7

Taittiriya Upanishad

तैत्तिरीयोपनिषद्

Krishna Yajurveda · 3 sections (vallis)

Three parts: the first is about pronunciation, study and the teacher's farewell advice ("Speak the truth, practise dharma," on the Yajurveda page). The second describes the Self covered by five sheaths. In the third, the boy Bhrigu asks his father Varuna, "Teach me Brahman." Varuna says only: "That from which beings are born, by which they live, and into which they return, seek that." Bhrigu meditates and discovers, step by step, that Brahman is food, then breath, then mind, then understanding, and finally bliss.

Brahman is truth, knowledge, infinite

Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1

सत्यं ज्ञानमनन्तं ब्रह्म ।

satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ brahma |

Word by word
सत्यं
truth, existence
ज्ञानम्
knowledge, consciousness
अनन्तं
infinite
ब्रह्म
(is) Brahman
Translation

Brahman is Truth, Knowledge, Infinity.

In simple words

The shortest definition of God in the Vedas: what is always real (truth), what is always aware (knowledge), and what has no limits (infinite). Later teachers add ananda, bliss, giving the famous phrase sat-chit-ananda.

📖 A story for this verse

Told in the Taittiriya Upanishad (Bhrigu Valli)

Young Bhrigu went to his father, the god Varuna, and said, "Teach me Brahman." Varuna did not give him an answer. He said, "Brahman is that from which all beings are born, by which they live, and into which they return. Find it through tapas, deep reflection."

Bhrigu reflected and came back: "Brahman is food. All beings come from food and live on food." Varuna said, "Reflect more." He came back: "Brahman is breath." "Reflect more." Then: "Brahman is mind." "More." Then: "Brahman is understanding." "More."

At last Bhrigu came back glowing: "Brahman is bliss. From bliss all beings are born, by bliss they live, and into bliss they return." This time Varuna said nothing more.

Each answer was true, but each was a layer. Brahman is truth, knowledge and the infinite, found by looking deeper and deeper.

8

Aitareya Upanishad

ऐतरेयोपनिषद्

Rigveda · 3 chapters

In the beginning there was only the Self. It thought, "Let me create the worlds," and created them, then the cosmic person, and then the senses and their gods: fire became speech, wind became breath, the sun became sight. Finally the Self entered the body through the crown of the head. The Upanishad ends with the Mahavakya prajñānaṃ brahma, "consciousness is Brahman."

9

Chandogya Upanishad

छान्दोग्योपनिषद्

Samaveda · 8 chapters

One of the two largest Upanishads, full of stories. Satyakama, a boy who did not know his father, went to a teacher and honestly repeated what his mother told him: "I don't know my lineage; I am Satyakama, son of Jabala." The teacher accepted him at once: "Only a true brahmin would speak such truth." Elsewhere the father Uddalaka teaches his son "Tat tvam asi," and the sage Sanatkumara teaches Narada that happiness lies only in the infinite. Several of its verses are explained on the Samaveda page.

10

Brihadaranyaka Upanishad

बृहदारण्यकोपनिषद्

Shukla Yajurveda · 6 chapters

"The great forest teaching," the longest Upanishad. Its hero is the sage Yajnavalkya, who wins a great debate at King Janaka's court, answering questions from many scholars including the woman philosopher Gargi. When he decides to renounce the world, he offers to divide his property between his two wives. His wife Maitreyi asks, "If the whole earth full of wealth were mine, would it make me immortal?" "No." "Then what use is it to me? Teach me what you know." It also contains "Asato ma sad gamaya" and "Aham brahmasmi" (both on the main page).

We love others for the sake of the Self

Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.5

न वा अरे पत्युः कामाय पतिः प्रियो भवत्यात्मनस्तु कामाय पतिः प्रियो भवति ।

na vā are patyuḥ kāmāya patiḥ priyo bhavaty ātmanas tu kāmāya patiḥ priyo bhavati |

Word by word
न वै अरे
not, my dear
पत्युः कामाय
for the husband's sake
पतिः प्रियः भवति
is the husband dear
आत्मनः तु कामाय
but for the sake of the Self
Translation

Truly, dear one, a husband is not dear for the husband's sake; he is dear for the sake of the Self.

In simple words

Yajnavalkya goes on: wife, children, wealth, the gods, the whole world, all are dear because of the Self. The love we feel for anyone is really the one Self recognising itself in another. So, he says, the Self is what must be seen, heard, reflected on and meditated upon.

📖 A story for this verse

Told in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad itself (2.4)

The sage Yajnavalkya decided to leave home and become a wandering monk. He called his two wives, Maitreyi and Katyayani. "Let me divide my property between you before I go."

Maitreyi asked a question no one expected. "If this whole earth, full of wealth, were mine, would it make me immortal?"

"No," said Yajnavalkya. "Your life would be like the life of the rich, but there is no hope of immortality through wealth."

"Then what would I do with what does not make me immortal?" said Maitreyi. "Tell me instead what you know."

Delighted, Yajnavalkya taught her: "A husband is dear, a wife is dear, children and wealth are dear, not for their own sake, but for the sake of the Self. It is the Self that must be seen, heard about, reflected on and meditated upon."

Da, da, da: the voice of thunder

Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.2.3

तदेतदेवैषा दैवी वागनुवदति स्तनयित्नुर्द द द इति ।
दाम्यत दत्त दयध्वमिति ॥

tad etad evaiṣā daivī vāg anuvadati stanayitnur da da da iti | dāmyata datta dayadhvam iti ||

Word by word
तत् एतत्
this very thing
एषा दैवी वाक्
this divine voice
अनुवदति
repeats
स्तनयित्नुः
the thunder
द द द इति
"Da, da, da"
दाम्यत
be self-controlled
दत्त
give
दयध्वम्
be compassionate
Translation

This is what the divine voice of thunder repeats: "Da! Da! Da!" Control yourselves, give, be compassionate.

In simple words

Gods, humans and demons each asked Prajapati for teaching. He said one syllable, "Da," to each. The gods, who were pleasure-loving, heard damyata, control yourselves. Humans, who were greedy, heard datta, give. Demons, who were cruel, heard dayadhvam, be kind. Each of us needs the lesson that fits our weakness. T. S. Eliot used these words to end his poem The Waste Land.

📖 A story for this verse

Told in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad itself (5.2)

The gods, the humans and the asuras were all children of Prajapati. When their studies were over, each group asked him for a final teaching.

To the gods he said one syllable: "Da." They thought, "He means damyata, control yourselves," because gods have every pleasure and little self-control. Prajapati said, "You understood."

To the humans he said, "Da." They thought, "He means datta, give," because humans tend to hold on to what they have. "You understood."

To the asuras he said, "Da." They thought, "He means dayadhvam, be compassionate," because asuras are often cruel. "You understood."

One sound, three lessons: each heard what they most needed. And the thunder still repeats it: Da! Da! Da!

Shvetashvatara Upanishad

श्वेताश्वतरोपनिषद्

Krishna Yajurveda · 6 chapters

Taught by the sage Shvetashvatara, this Upanishad brings together knowledge, yoga and devotion to a personal God, whom it calls Rudra or Shiva. It gives some of the earliest instructions for meditation (posture, breath, a quiet place), and ends with one of the earliest uses of the word bhakti, devotion to God and to the guru (6.23). It also repeats the two-birds verse.

Children of immortality

Shvetashvatara Upanishad 2.5

शृण्वन्तु विश्वे अमृतस्य पुत्राः ।
आ ये धामानि दिव्यानि तस्थुः ॥

śṛṇvantu viśve amṛtasya putrā ā ye dhāmāni divyāni tasthuḥ ||

Word by word
शृण्वन्तु
may they hear
विश्वे
all
अमृतस्य पुत्राः
children of immortality
आ ये
those who
धामानि दिव्यानि
the divine realms
तस्थुः
dwell
Translation

Listen, all you children of immortality, even you who dwell in the heavenly realms!

In simple words

Swami Vivekananda quoted this at the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893, telling the audience: you are not sinners, you are children of immortal bliss. Every human being has divine worth.
