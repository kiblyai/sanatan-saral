Hinduism in 30 seconds

  • Sanatan Dharma, "the eternal way," is the traditional name of Hinduism. It has no single founder or single book.
  • Its oldest scriptures are the four Vedas; their philosophical end, the Upanishads, teaches that your true Self (Atman) is one with ultimate reality (Brahman).
  • The Bhagavad Gita shows how to live this: do your duty without selfishness, with devotion and understanding.
  • The Ramayana, Mahabharata and Puranas teach the same truths through stories, and give us our festivals.
  • Core ideas: dharma (right living), karma (actions have results), rebirth, and moksha (freedom). Many paths lead to the same truth.

What is Sanatan Dharma (Hinduism)?

Sanātana means "eternal, without beginning or end." Dharma is harder to translate. It means the natural order that holds the world together, and also the right way for a person to live within it: doing your duty, being truthful, and not causing harm. So Sanatan Dharma means "the eternal way of living rightly." It is the traditional name for what the world today calls Hinduism.

Unlike many religions, it has no single founder, no single holy book, and no single head. It grew over thousands of years through the insights of sages called rishis, and it welcomes many paths to the same truth. You can worship God with form, without form, through knowledge, through action, or through love, and all of these are accepted.

This guide follows the traditional view where it explains beliefs, and gives the dates historians use where it talks about history. Different schools (Vedanta, Arya Samaj, Vaishnava, Shaiva, Shakta and others) interpret many verses differently. The translations here follow the commonly accepted meaning.

The map of the scriptures

Shruti and Smriti: what was heard, what was remembered

All Hindu scriptures fall into two families. Shruti ("that which is heard") is revealed knowledge that the rishis are believed to have heard in deep meditation. It is the highest authority. Smriti ("that which is remembered") was written by human authors to explain and apply Shruti through law, story and poetry.

Shruti

Revealed · highest authority

  • The four Vedas: Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva
  • Each Veda has four layers: Samhita (hymns), Brahmana (ritual), Aranyaka (forest reflections), Upanishad (philosophy)

Smriti

Remembered · explains the Vedas

  • Itihasa (epics): Ramayana, Mahabharata (which contains the Bhagavad Gita)
  • Puranas: 18 major Puranas of stories about God, creation and kings
  • Dharma Shastras: codes of conduct, such as the Manusmriti
  • Vedangas: six helper sciences: phonetics, ritual, grammar, etymology, metre, astronomy
  • Darshanas: six schools of philosophy: Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Sankhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, Vedanta

The root of everything

The Four Vedas

Veda comes from the Sanskrit root vid, "to know." The Vedas are the oldest texts of India and among the oldest in the world. For more than three thousand years they were passed down by memory, from teacher to student, with special recitation methods that kept every syllable and tone exact. UNESCO recognised this Vedic chanting tradition as an intangible heritage of humanity in 2008.

Rigveda

10 mandalas · 1,028 hymns · about 10,550 mantras

The Veda of praise. Hymns to the forces of nature as divine powers: Agni (fire), Indra (strength, rain), Surya (sun), Varuna (cosmic order), Ushas (dawn). It also asks deep questions about how the universe began. The oldest of the four.

Explore the Rigveda →

Yajurveda

Two branches: Shukla (white) and Krishna (black) · Shukla has 40 chapters

The Veda of action. A handbook for the priest who performs the yajna (fire ritual): what to say and do at each step. Its last chapter is the famous Isha Upanishad.

Explore the Yajurveda →

Samaveda

About 1,875 verses · nearly all taken from the Rigveda

The Veda of song. Rigvedic verses set to melody, sung by the udgatri priest. Indian classical music traces its roots here. In the Gita, Krishna says "among the Vedas, I am the Samaveda" (10.22).

Explore the Samaveda →

Atharvaveda

20 books · about 730 hymns · about 6,000 mantras

The Veda of daily life. Prayers for health, healing, long life, a happy home, farming and protection, along with hymns about the earth and the nation. Ayurveda draws on it.

The Gayatri Mantra

Rigveda 3.62.10

ॐ भूर्भुवः स्वः ।
तत्सवितुर्वरेण्यं भर्गो देवस्य धीमहि ।
धियो यो नः प्रचोदयात् ॥

oṃ bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ | tat savitur vareṇyaṃ bhargo devasya dhīmahi | dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt ||

Word by word
भूः भुवः स्वः
earth, sky, heaven (the three worlds)
तत्
that
सवितुः
of Savitri, the Sun, the giver of life
वरेण्यम्
most excellent, worth choosing
भर्गः
radiance, purifying light
देवस्य

धीमहि

धियः

Translation

We meditate on the glorious light of the divine Sun, who is worthy of worship. May that light inspire our minds.

In simple words

This is a prayer for a clear, good mind, not for money or things. Just as the sun lights up the world, we ask the divine light to light up our thinking so we make good choices. The opening words "Om bhur bhuvah svah" were added later in tradition and are not part of the Rigveda verse itself.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Valmiki Ramayana (Bala Kanda)

The Gayatri Mantra was first seen by the sage Vishvamitra. But he was not born a sage. He was King Kaushika, a mighty warrior.

One day, with his army, he visited the ashram of the sage Vasishtha. Vasishtha fed thousands of soldiers a royal feast, all from his wish-fulfilling cow, Shabala. The king wanted the cow. When Vasishtha refused, the king tried to take her by force, and lost. All his weapons failed against the sage's quiet spiritual strength.

"Real power is the power of the spirit," Kaushika realised. He gave up his kingdom and began long years of tapas. He fell many times, through anger and pride, and rose again each time. At last even Vasishtha honoured him as a brahmarshi, the highest of sages.

The mantra he gave the world asks for exactly what changed him: "May that divine light inspire our minds."

The very first verse of the Rigveda

Rigveda 1.1.1

अग्निमीळे पुरोहितं यज्ञस्य देवमृत्विजम् ।
होतारं रत्नधातमम् ॥

agnim īḷe purohitaṃ yajñasya devam ṛtvijam | hotāraṃ ratnadhātamam ||

Word by word
अग्निम् ईळे
I praise Agni (fire)
पुरोहितम्
placed in front, the leader
यज्ञस्य देवम्
the divine one of the sacrifice
ऋत्विजम्
the priest who acts at the right season
होतारम्
the one who calls the gods
रत्नधातमम्
the greatest giver of treasures
Translation

I praise Agni, the one placed in front, the god of the sacrifice, the priest, the caller of the gods, the greatest giver of treasures.

In simple words

The oldest Veda opens with fire. Fire was the link between people and the divine: whatever is offered into it is carried upward. Fire also stands for energy, effort and the light of knowledge, which is why we still light a diya or a havan at every important moment.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Kena Upanishad

The Rigveda opens by praising Agni, the god of fire. The Kena Upanishad tells a story about where Agni's power really comes from.

After a great victory, the gods became proud: "We won this." Then a strange, shining being, a Yaksha, appeared before them. Agni went forward boldly. "I am Agni. I can burn up everything on earth." The Yaksha placed a single blade of dry grass before him. "Burn this." Agni rushed at it with all his might, and could not burn it.

Vayu, the wind, went next. "I can blow away anything." He could not move that blade of grass.

Then the goddess Uma appeared and explained: the Yaksha was Brahman, the Supreme. The gods' victory, and Agni's fire, were Brahman's power working through them. Agni is honoured first in the Rigveda, and Agni himself learned humility.

Truth is one, the wise call it by many names

Rigveda 1.164.46

इन्द्रं मित्रं वरुणमग्निमाहुरथो दिव्यः स सुपर्णो गरुत्मान् ।
एकं सद्विप्रा बहुधा वदन्त्यग्निं यमं मातरिश्वानमाहुः ॥

indraṃ mitraṃ varuṇam agnim āhur atho divyaḥ sa suparṇo garutmān | ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadanty agniṃ yamaṃ mātariśvānam āhuḥ ||

Word by word
इन्द्रं मित्रं वरुणम् अग्निम्
Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni
आहुः
they call (it)
दिव्यः सुपर्णः गरुत्मान्
the heavenly, beautiful-winged Garutman
एकं सत्
the one truth, the one reality
विप्राः
the wise, the seers
बहुधा वदन्ति
speak of in many ways
यमं मातरिश्वानम्
Yama, Matarishvan (wind)
Translation

They call it Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, and the heavenly winged Garutman. The truth is one; the wise speak of it in many ways. They call it Agni, Yama, Matarishvan.

In simple words

The many gods are many faces of one divine reality, like one person being called "father," "son," and "teacher" by different people. This single line is the root of Hindu tolerance: if truth is one and names are many, there is no need to fight over names.

📖 A story for this verse

A traditional folk tale

Six blind men were taken to meet an elephant for the first time. Each touched one part.

"An elephant is like a wall," said the one who touched its side. "No, like a snake," said the one holding the trunk. "Like a spear," said the one at the tusk. "Like a tree trunk," said the one hugging a leg. "Like a fan," said the one at the ear. "Like a rope," said the one with the tail.

They argued loudly, each sure he alone was right. A wise man passing by said, "You are all right, and you are all wrong. Each of you has touched one part. The elephant is all of these together."

The Rigveda said it thousands of years ago: the truth is one; the wise call it by many names.

Hymn of Creation (Nasadiya Sukta)

Translation

1. Then there was neither non-existence nor existence. There was no air, nor the sky beyond it. What covered it? Where? Under whose protection? Was there water, deep and unfathomable?

7. From where this creation arose, whether it was made or not made, only the one who watches over it in the highest heaven knows. Or perhaps even he does not know.

In simple words

Three thousand years ago, a rishi asked the same question scientists ask today: what was there before the universe? Instead of giving a fixed answer, he ends with honest wonder. The Vedas encourage asking questions, not just believing.

📖 A story for this verse

An everyday story

Under the night sky on the terrace, eight-year-old Aarav asked his grandfather, "Dadaji, what was there before the stars? Before everything?"

Grandfather smiled. "Scientists are still finding out. And do you know, our oldest book asked the very same question?" He recited the Nasadiya Sukta: there was neither being nor non-being, no air, no sky. Who knows where all this came from?

"And what's the answer?" asked Aarav.

"The poem ends: perhaps the one who watches over it from the highest heaven knows. Or perhaps even he does not know."

Aarav's eyes grew wide. "The Veda says it doesn't know?"

"It says it is honest," Grandfather said. "Wonder and humility are where wisdom begins. Keep asking."

Purusha Sukta: the Cosmic Being

Rigveda 10.90.1

सहस्रशीर्षा पुरुषः सहस्राक्षः सहस्रपात् ।
स भूमिं विश्वतो वृत्वात्यतिष्ठद्दशाङ्गुलम् ॥

sahasraśīrṣā puruṣaḥ sahasrākṣaḥ sahasrapāt | sa bhūmiṃ viśvato vṛtvāty atiṣṭhad daśāṅgulam ||

Word by word
सहस्रशीर्षा
with a thousand heads
पुरुषः
the Cosmic Person
सहस्राक्षः सहस्रपात्
thousand-eyed, thousand-footed
भूमिं विश्वतः वृत्वा
covering the earth on all sides
अति अतिष्ठत् दशाङ्गुलम्
stood beyond it by ten fingers' breadth
Translation

The Cosmic Being has a thousand heads, a thousand eyes, a thousand feet. He pervades the earth on every side and extends beyond it by ten fingers.

In simple words

"A thousand" means "countless." All beings' heads, eyes and feet are part of one divine whole. God is inside the universe and also greater than it. This hymn is recited at almost every puja and havan.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Bhagavata Purana

One day little Krishna's friends ran to his mother Yashoda. "Krishna has been eating mud!"

Yashoda took his hand and scolded him. "Open your mouth! Let me see." Krishna said, "I haven't eaten anything, Mother. They are lying." Then he opened his mouth wide.

Yashoda looked in, and froze. Inside her little son's mouth she saw the whole universe: the sky and the stars, the sun and the moon, mountains, oceans and islands, the earth itself. She even saw the village of Vraja, and herself standing there looking into his mouth.

Trembling, she wondered whether she was dreaming. Then, by his loving will, she forgot what she had seen, picked him up and held him close as her child once more.

The Purusha Sukta speaks of a Being with a thousand heads and eyes who fills the whole world and goes beyond it. Yashoda saw that Being as a toddler with mud on his lips.

Mahamrityunjaya Mantra

Rigveda 7.59.12

त्र्यम्बकं यजामहे सुगन्धिं पुष्टिवर्धनम् ।
उर्वारुकमिव बन्धनान्मृत्योर्मुक्षीय माऽमृतात् ॥

tryambakaṃ yajāmahe sugandhiṃ puṣṭivardhanam | urvārukam iva bandhanān mṛtyor mukṣīya māmṛtāt ||

Word by word
त्र्यम्बकम्
the three-eyed one (Shiva)
यजामहे
we worship
सुगन्धिम्
fragrant, auspicious
पुष्टिवर्धनम्
who increases nourishment and strength
उर्वारुकम् इव

Translation

We worship the three-eyed Lord, who is fragrant and who nourishes all. As a ripe cucumber is freed from its vine, may he free us from death, but not from immortality.

In simple words

A ripe cucumber falls from the vine easily, with no pain. The prayer asks for that kind of ripeness in life: to grow fully, let go of fear, and pass on peacefully when the time comes. People chant it for healing and courage.

📖 A story for this verse

From Puranic tradition (the story of Markandeya)

The sage Mrikandu and his wife longed for a child. Shiva offered them a choice: a son who was dull but would live long, or a son who was wise and good but would live only sixteen years. They chose the wise son. He was named Markandeya.

Markandeya grew up gentle, learned and devoted to Shiva. As his sixteenth birthday came near, his parents could not hide their grief, and he learned the truth. He went to a Shiva linga and prayed with his whole heart.

When the day came, Yama, the lord of death, arrived and threw his noose. Markandeya threw his arms around the linga and held on. The noose fell around both the boy and the linga. At that moment Shiva burst out of the linga, struck Yama down, and blessed Markandeya to remain sixteen forever.

Tradition links the Mahamrityunjaya Mantra, the "great victory over death", with this story. It does not ask to live forever in the body; it asks to be freed from fear of death, like a ripe fruit freed gently from its vine.

Walk together, speak together

Rigveda 10.191.2

संगच्छध्वं संवदध्वं सं वो मनांसि जानताम् ।
देवा भागं यथा पूर्वे संजानाना उपासते ॥

saṃ gacchadhvaṃ saṃ vadadhvaṃ saṃ vo manāṃsi jānatām | devā bhāgaṃ yathā pūrve saṃjānānā upāsate ||

Word by word
सं गच्छध्वम्
move together
सं वदध्वम्
speak together
सं वः मनांसि जानताम्
may your minds understand together
देवाः
the gods
भागं
their share

agreeing, in harmony

Translation

Walk together, speak together, let your minds be in harmony, just as the gods of old shared their portion in unity.

In simple words

This is the closing hymn of the whole Rigveda, and it is about teamwork. Families, villages and nations do well when people move in the same direction, talk to each other, and understand each other.

📖 A story for this verse

A traditional folk tale

An old farmer had four sons who quarrelled all the time. Worried, he called them to his bedside and gave each a single stick. "Break it." Each snapped his stick easily.

Then he tied four sticks together in a bundle. "Now break this." Each son tried with all his strength. None could break the bundle.

"Alone, each of you is like one stick," the farmer said. "Anyone can break you. Together, no one can."

The sons understood. They stopped quarrelling and worked the farm as one, and it did well.

The very last hymn of the Rigveda prays: walk together, speak together, let your minds be one.

The first song of the Samaveda

Samaveda 1 (also Rigveda 6.16.10)

अग्न आ याहि वीतये गृणानो हव्यदातये ।
नि होता सत्सि बर्हिषि ॥

agna ā yāhi vītaye gṛṇāno havyadātaye | ni hotā satsi barhiṣi ||

Word by word
अग्ने

आ याहि

वीतये

गृणानः

Translation

O Agni, come to the feast, praised, to carry our offerings. Sit down as our priest on the sacred grass.

In simple words

This shows how the Samaveda works: it takes a Rigveda verse and turns it into a song. The same words, sung with melody, become a different kind of prayer, one you feel as much as understand.

📖 A story for this verse

An everyday story

Before Diwali puja, little Isha watched her grandmother light the lamp, ring the bell and softly sing, "Come, O Lord, come and sit with us."

"Why do we call God first, Dadi? Isn't God everywhere already?"

"When a dear guest comes, do we just let them stand at the door?" asked Dadi. "We say, come in, sit down, have some water. Calling God is like that. It is not for God's sake; it is to turn our own hearts towards the guest."

She showed Isha the first song of the Samaveda in a little book. "Look. Thousands of years ago, our ancestors began their great songbook the same way: Agni, come, sit down on the sacred grass with us."

From then on, Isha always sang the invitation herself.

Earth is my mother

Atharvaveda 12.1.12 (part)

माता भूमिः पुत्रो अहं पृथिव्याः ।

mātā bhūmiḥ putro ahaṃ pṛthivyāḥ |

Word by word
माता
mother
भूमिः
the Earth
पुत्रः
son, child
अहम्

पृथिव्याः

Translation

The Earth is my mother, and I am her child.

In simple words

The Bhumi Sukta has 63 verses of love for the earth: its rivers, mountains, plants and all its peoples. If the earth is our mother, we should not harm her. It is one of the earliest environmental prayers in the world. That is also why many Hindus touch the ground before stepping out of bed in the morning.

📖 A story for this verse

An everyday story

Every morning, before stepping out of bed, Riya's grandmother touched the floor and then her forehead, whispering a short Sanskrit verse.

"What are you saying, Ba?" Riya asked.

"I am saying sorry to Mother Earth," Ba said. "The verse means: O Goddess, whose clothes are the oceans and whose body is the mountains, forgive me for touching you with my feet."

Riya laughed. "The earth doesn't mind!"

"Maybe not. But I mind," said Ba. "She gives us food, water and a place to stand, every day, and asks for nothing. A child who remembers that will not waste water, throw plastic in the river, or cut a tree for no reason."

The next morning, Riya touched the floor too. The Atharvaveda says it simply: the Earth is my mother, and I am her child.

In simple words

Keep an open mind. Good ideas can come from anywhere and anyone, so be ready to learn from all of them.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Bhagavata Purana

King Yadu once met a young wandering sage who was always joyful, though he owned nothing. "Who taught you to be so happy?" the king asked.

"I have twenty-four teachers," the sage, Dattatreya, replied, "and none of them knows it.

"The earth taught me patience: people dig and trample her, and she still feeds them. The wind taught me to move among all things without clinging to any. The ocean taught me to stay calm whether rivers pour in or not. The honeybee taught me to take a little goodness from every flower. The moth, flying into the flame, taught me what happens when desire blinds us. Even a child taught me how to be happy with nothing."

He went on through all twenty-four, from the sun and moon to a pigeon and a python.

"May noble thoughts come to us from every side," prays the Rigveda. Dattatreya lived that prayer: the whole world was his school.

The philosophy of the Vedas

The Upanishads

Upa-ni-shad means "to sit down near," the way a student sat close to a teacher to hear deep truths. Over 200 Upanishads are known; tradition lists 108, and about 11 are considered principal: Isha, Kena, Katha, Prashna, Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Chandogya, Brihadaranyaka and Shvetashvatara. Their big question is: Who am I really, and what is the reality behind everything?

God dwells in everything

Isha Upanishad 1 (Yajurveda 40.1)

ईशा वास्यमिदं सर्वं यत्किञ्च जगत्यां जगत् ।
तेन त्यक्तेन भुञ्जीथा मा गृधः कस्यस्विद्धनम् ॥

Word by word

Translation

All this, whatever moves in the world, is dwelt in by the Lord. Enjoy it by letting go. Do not covet; whose is wealth, after all?

In simple words

Use what you need and enjoy life, but don't cling or grab. Everything belongs to God; we are only caretakers for a while. Mahatma Gandhi said that if all the Upanishads were lost and only this one verse survived, Hinduism would live on.

📖 A story for this verse

A traditional folk tale

In some villages, people used to catch monkeys with a simple trick. They placed a few nuts inside a pot with a narrow neck and tied the pot to a tree.

A monkey came, slipped its hand in and grabbed a fistful of nuts. Now its fist was too big to come out. The monkey pulled and screeched, but it would not open its hand. It could have walked away free at any moment, simply by letting go.

The monkey was not caught by the pot. It was caught by its own grip.

The Isha Upanishad says: everything here belongs to the Lord. Enjoy it by letting go. Do not grasp at what others have. We can use and enjoy the good things of life, as long as our fist stays open.

Lead me from the unreal to the real. Lead me from darkness to light. Lead me from death to immortality.

In simple words

Three steps of growth: from false ideas to truth, from ignorance to knowledge, from fear of death to knowing the deathless soul. It is a prayer every student, and every person, can say.

📖 A story for this verse

An everyday story

When the power went out during a storm, six-year-old Kabir froze in the dark hallway. "Mumma! I can't see anything!"

His mother came with a small diya. In its warm light the hallway was just the hallway again: the same shoes, the same coat on the hook. Nothing had changed except that now he could see.

"The scary things weren't real, were they?" Kabir whispered.

"Most of what scares us in the dark isn't," his mother said. "Our ancestors had a prayer for that." Together they said it: asato ma sad gamaya, tamaso ma jyotir gamaya, mrityor ma amritam gamaya.

"Lead me from the unreal to the real, from darkness to light, from death to immortality." The diya does for the hallway what this prayer asks God to do for our minds.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Vishnu Purana and Vedic tradition

The sage Vaishampayana taught the Yajurveda to many students. His brightest student was Yajnavalkya. One day the teacher and student had a bitter quarrel, and each was too proud to give way.

In anger the teacher said, "Give back everything I taught you!" Yajnavalkya, just as angry, brought up all the learning he had received, and left. The other students, taking the form of partridges (tittiri), picked up that knowledge. Tradition says this is how the Taittiriya branch of the Yajurveda got its name.

Yajnavalkya went on to worship the Sun and received a new Yajurveda. But a priceless bond between teacher and student had been broken in a single angry moment.

That is why teacher and student chant this peace prayer together before every lesson: may we learn together, may our learning shine, and may we never hate each other.

Arise, awake

Translation

In simple words

Swami Vivekananda made this line famous: "Arise, awake, and stop not till the goal is reached." Don't sleep through life. Wake up, find good teachers, and keep learning.

📖 A story for this verse

From the life of Swami Vivekananda

In 1892 a young monk named Vivekananda had walked the length of India. Everywhere he saw both deep wisdom and deep poverty, and it troubled him.

At Kanyakumari, the southern tip of India, he swam out to a rock in the sea and sat there in meditation for three days. He came back with a clear purpose: to wake people up, to give them back their self-confidence and to serve the poor.

The next year he travelled to America and spoke at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago, opening with "Sisters and brothers of America". When he returned to India, his message to the young was this line from the Katha Upanishad: "Arise! Awake! And stop not till the goal is reached."

Today a memorial stands on that rock, and his birthday, 12 January, is celebrated as National Youth Day in India.

The four Great Sayings (Mahavakyas)

One short sentence from an Upanishad of each Veda sums up the whole teaching: your innermost Self (Atman) and the ultimate reality (Brahman) are the same.

प्रज्ञानं ब्रह्म

prajñānaṃ brahma · Aitareya Up., Rigveda

"Consciousness is Brahman." The awareness by which you know anything is itself the divine.

अहं ब्रह्मास्मि

ahaṃ brahmāsmi · Brihadaranyaka Up., Yajurveda

"I am Brahman." Not the body or ego, but the true Self within.

तत्त्वमसि

tat tvam asi · Chandogya Up., Samaveda

"You are That." A father tells his son Shvetaketu: the subtle essence of everything is your own Self.

अयमात्मा ब्रह्म

ayam ātmā brahma · Mandukya Up., Atharvaveda

"This Self is Brahman." The same truth, said once more.

The big ideas

Core ideas, explained simply

धर्म Dharma

Right action, duty and the natural order. A river's dharma is to flow, fire's is to burn, and a person's is to do their duties honestly and kindly. Dharma changes with your role: a parent, a student and a doctor each have their own.

कर्म Karma

Every action has a result, like a seed that grows into a plant. Good actions bring good results, harmful ones bring suffering, in this life or a later one. Karma is not fate: your choices today shape your tomorrow.

आत्मा Atman

Your true Self: not the body, not the mind, but the pure awareness inside that never dies. The body is like clothing the soul wears.

ब्रह्म Brahman

The one infinite reality behind all things. When given form and worshipped with love, the same reality is called Ishvara or Bhagavan: Vishnu, Shiva, Devi and others.

संसार व पुनर्जन्म Samsara

The soul passes through many births. Its karma decides the next life, much as today's actions decide tomorrow's circumstances.

मोक्ष Moksha

Freedom from the cycle of birth and death: knowing your true Self and resting in lasting peace and joy. It is the final goal of life.

ऋत Rta

The cosmic order the Rigveda speaks of: the sun rises, seasons turn, rivers flow. Living by dharma means living in tune with that order.

वसुधैव कुटुम्बकम् Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam

"The whole world is one family" (Maha Upanishad). Small minds divide people into "mine" and "theirs"; generous hearts see everyone as family.

Four goals of life (Purusharthas)

Sanatan Dharma does not ask everyone to renounce the world. It accepts four healthy goals: Dharma (living rightly), Artha (earning wealth honestly), Kama (enjoying love, art and pleasure), and Moksha (spiritual freedom). Wealth and pleasure are good, as long as they are pursued within dharma.

Do your work, let go of the result

Gita 2.47

कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन ।
मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि ॥

karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana | mā karmaphalahetur bhūr mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi ||

Word by word
कर्मणि एव
in action alone
अधिकारः ते
is your right
मा फलेषु कदाचन
never in its fruits
मा कर्मफलहेतुः भूः
do not make the result your motive
मा ते सङ्गः अस्तु अकर्मणि
nor be attached to doing nothing
Translation

You have a right to your work, but never to its fruits. Do not let the result be your motive, and do not become attached to inaction.

In simple words

A student should study with full attention, without being frozen by worry about marks. Put your whole heart into the effort, which you control. The outcome depends on many things, so don't let fear of it stop you. And don't use this as an excuse to do nothing.

📖 A story for this verse

An everyday story

Meera had her first music recital. For weeks she kept asking, "What if I forget? What if nobody claps?" Her practice got worse, because her mind was always on the result.

Her grandmother said, "The claps are not in your hands. The practice is. Give each day's practice your full heart, and leave the rest."

Meera stopped counting days to the recital and simply played well every evening. On the day, she slipped once, smiled and carried on. She came home happy, not because of the applause, but because she had given her best.

That is the verse: do your work fully; the result is not yours to hold.

The soul changes bodies like clothes

📖 A story for this verse

An everyday story

After Nana passed away, ten-year-old Tara would not stop crying. "Where did he go? Is he gone forever?"

Her mother opened Nana's cupboard and took out his old brown sweater, worn thin at the elbows. "Nana wore this every winter for twenty years. When it got too old, he bought a new one. Did Nana disappear when he took this sweater off?"

"No," Tara said slowly. "He was still Nana."

"Our tradition says the body is like this sweater," her mother said, "and the real person, the soul, is the one who wears it. In the Gita, Krishna says the soul puts down a worn-out body the way we put down old clothes, and takes a new one. The sweater is here. Nana is not in the sweater."

Tara held the sweater close. It still smelled of him. She missed him just as much, but she was not so afraid any more.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Bhagavata Purana

The demon king Hiranyakashipu won a boon that no man or animal could kill him, neither by day nor by night, neither inside nor outside, neither on earth nor in the sky, by no weapon. He declared himself god and punished anyone who prayed to Vishnu.

His own young son, Prahlada, would not stop. The king tried to have him thrown from cliffs, trampled by elephants and burned in fire, but Prahlada stayed calm and kept saying Vishnu's name.

At last the king struck a pillar and roared, "Is your Vishnu in this too?" The pillar burst open. Out came Narasimha, half man and half lion. At dusk, on the threshold, holding the king on his lap and using only his claws, he ended Hiranyakashipu's cruelty, without breaking a single word of the boon.

When good people are crushed and wrong grows strong, the Lord comes. That is the promise of this verse.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Bhagavata Purana

Five-year-old Dhruva tried to climb onto the lap of his father, King Uttanapada. His stepmother Suruchi pulled him away: "Only my son may sit there. If you want that place, pray to God to be born as my child!"

Dhruva ran crying to his mother Suniti. She did not blame anyone. She said, "Only the Lord can give you a place no one can take away."

The little boy walked into the forest. The sage Narada tested him, then taught him a mantra. Dhruva meditated with such determination that Vishnu himself appeared and gave him a place that would never move. Tradition says he shines today as the Pole Star.

No one lifted Dhruva up; he lifted himself. As the verse says, your own mind can be your best friend.

Itihasa: "so it happened"

Ramayana and Mahabharata

The Vedas teach through mantras; the epics teach through stories. Most people in India first learn dharma not from the Vedas but from these two great tales, told by grandparents, performed in Ramlila, and watched on television.

Read both epics book by book, with family tree →

Ramayana

Sage Valmiki · 7 kandas · about 24,000 verses

Prince Rama gives up his throne to keep his father's promise and spends 14 years in the forest with Sita and Lakshmana. Ravana kidnaps Sita; with the help of Hanuman and an army of vanaras, Rama rescues her and returns to Ayodhya. Diwali celebrates that return.

LessonRama is maryada purushottam, the ideal person who keeps his word and does his duty even when it hurts. Hanuman shows devotion and strength; Sita shows courage and dignity.

Tulsidas's Hindi Ramcharitmanas (16th century) is the version most North Indian homes read.

Mahabharata

Sage Vyasa · 18 parvas · about 100,000 verses

The longest poem in the world. Two sets of cousins, the five Pandavas and the hundred Kauravas, quarrel over a kingdom. After cheating, exile and failed peace talks, they fight the 18-day war at Kurukshetra. The Gita is spoken just before the battle begins.

LessonLife is complicated: good people make mistakes and dharma is often hard to see. The epic says of itself: "what is here may be found elsewhere; what is not here is nowhere."

The Puranas

Purana means "ancient." These books retell Vedic truths through stories of gods, avatars, saints and kings, so that everyone can understand them. They describe creation and destruction of the universe, family lines of kings, the ten avatars of Vishnu, the glory of Shiva and of Devi, holy places, fasts and festivals. Most of the stories behind Hindu festivals come from the Puranas.

The ten avatars of Vishnu (Dashavatara)

Many people notice that the order reads like a story of life developing: from a fish in water, to an amphibian, to a land animal, to half-human, to a small human, and on to full human beings.

  1. Matsya, the fish, saves the Vedas from the flood
  2. Kurma, the tortoise, holds up Mount Mandara
  3. Varaha, the boar, lifts the earth from the ocean
  4. Narasimha, man-lion, protects Prahlada
  5. Vamana, the dwarf, humbles King Bali
  6. Parashurama, the warrior-sage
  7. Rama, the ideal king
  8. Krishna, teacher of the Gita
  9. Buddha (in many lists) or Balarama
  10. Kalki, yet to come at the end of Kali Yuga

Living it

Daily practice, life rituals and festivals

Daily practice

Waking early, bathing, a few minutes of prayer or sandhya, lighting a lamp, chanting a mantra like the Gayatri, yoga and meditation, respecting elders, and offering food to God before eating.

The five daily duties

Every householder owes something each day to five: to the sages (by studying), to the gods (by worship), to ancestors (by remembering them), to other people (by feeding guests), and to animals and nature.

The 16 samskaras

Sacred ceremonies that mark each stage of life, from before birth to after death. Well-known ones: namakarana (naming), annaprashana (first solid food), mundan (first haircut), upanayana (sacred thread, start of study), vivaha (marriage) and antyeshti (last rites).

Seva and daan

Serving others and giving to those in need are forms of worship. The Gita says the best gift is given at the right time, to the right person, expecting nothing back (17.20).
