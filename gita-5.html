Renunciation of Action

5.1 · Arjuna

Gita 5.1

संन्यासं कर्मणां कृष्ण पुनर्योगं च शंससि ।
यच्छ्रेय एतयोरेकं तन्मे ब्रूहि सुनिश्चितम् ॥

saṃnyāsaṃ karmaṇāṃ kṛṣṇa punaryogaṃ ca śaṃsasi | yacchreya etayorekaṃ tanme brūhi suniścitam ||

Translation

Arjuna said: Krishna, you praise giving up action, and then again you praise the yoga of action. Tell me for certain which of the two is better.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Mahabharata (Shanti Parva)

King Janaka of Mithila ruled a rich kingdom, yet the sages called him a rajarshi, a king who was also a sage. He did all his royal duties with great care, but his heart was not tied to any of it.

The Mahabharata remembers a famous line of his, and storytellers set it in a scene: while Janaka was deep in a discussion of the Self, word came that his capital was on fire. Janaka said, "If Mithila burns, nothing of mine is burnt." He did not mean he did not care about his people; he meant that what is truly "his", the Self, cannot be burnt.

Like a lotus leaf that lives in water but is never wet, Janaka lived in the middle of wealth and power without being stained by them.

📖 A story for this verse

A traditional story of Adi Shankaracharya (Manisha Panchakam)

In Kashi, the great teacher Shankaracharya was walking back from his bath in the Ganga with his students. A man from the lowest caste, with four dogs, stood in the narrow lane. Shankara's students called out, "Move aside! Keep your distance!"

The man did not move. He asked calmly, "Whom do you want to move? This body made of food, from another body made of food? Or the pure awareness inside me, from the same awareness inside you?"

Shankara stopped. He had taught this truth for years, and here it was being taught back to him. He bowed to the man and composed five verses, saying that whoever truly sees the one Self in everyone is his teacher, whatever his birth.

That is the equal eye of the wise that this verse describes.
