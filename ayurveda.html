Ayurveda

The knowledge of life

Ayurveda means "the knowledge (veda) of life (ayus)". It is India's traditional system of health, linked to the Atharvaveda: the Sushruta Samhita calls it an upanga, a supporting limb, of the Atharvaveda. It looks at the whole person: body, mind, food, sleep, season and surroundings.

The two aims of Ayurveda

Charaka Samhita, Sutrasthana 30.26

प्रयोजनं चास्य स्वस्थस्य स्वास्थ्यरक्षणम् आतुरस्य विकारप्रशमनं च ॥

prayojanaṃ cāsya svasthasya svāsthya-rakṣaṇam āturasya vikāra-praśamanaṃ ca ||

Translation

Its purpose is to protect the health of the healthy, and to relieve the illness of the sick.

In simple words

Notice that staying healthy comes first. Most of Ayurveda is about daily habits, food and seasons, not pills.

Who is truly healthy?

Sushruta Samhita, Sutrasthana 15

समदोषः समाग्निश्च समधातुमलक्रियः ।
प्रसन्नात्मेन्द्रियमनाः स्वस्थ इत्यभिधीयते ॥

samadoṣaḥ samāgniś ca sama-dhātu-mala-kriyaḥ | prasannātmendriya-manāḥ svastha ity abhidhīyate ||

Translation

One whose doshas are balanced, whose digestive fire is balanced, whose tissues and wastes work properly, and whose soul, senses and mind are cheerful, is called healthy.

In simple words

Two thousand years ago, health already meant a happy mind as well as a working body.

Origins

Dhanvantari and the three great books

Dhanvantari, the god of healing. When the gods and asuras churned the ocean of milk, Dhanvantari rose from the waters carrying the pot of amrit, the nectar of immortality (Bhagavata Purana 8.8). He is honoured as the founder of Ayurveda. His day, Dhanvantari Trayodashi, is Dhanteras, the first day of Diwali. India marked National Ayurveda Day on Dhanteras from 2016, and in 2025 fixed it on 23 September each year.

Charaka Samhita

Medicine

The great book of internal medicine, based on the teachings of the sage Atreya and edited by Charaka about 2,000 years ago. It covers diet, daily routine, diagnosis and the ethics of a good physician.

Sushruta Samhita

Surgery

The great book of surgery. It describes many surgical instruments, how to train surgeons by practising on fruits and vegetables, and how to rebuild a damaged nose using a flap of skin from the cheek, an early form of plastic surgery.

Ashtanga Hridaya

A summary

Written by Vagbhata around the 7th century, it brings the teachings of Charaka and Sushruta together in clear verse. It is still the main textbook in many Ayurveda colleges, especially in Kerala.

Core ideas

Five elements, three doshas

  • Five elements (pancha mahabhuta): space, air, fire, water and earth make up everything, including our bodies.
  • Three doshas: the elements work in the body as three forces, vata, pitta and kapha. Everyone has all three.
  • Prakriti: your natural mix of the three, set at birth. Ayurveda says health is staying close to your own balance, not someone else's.
  • Agni: the digestive fire. Strong, steady agni turns food into health; weak agni is seen as the start of many problems.

🌬️

Vata

Air and space · movement

Governs all movement: breath, heartbeat, nerves and thought. In balance: lively, creative, quick. Out of balance: dry skin, worry, poor sleep, irregular appetite.

🔥

Pitta

Fire and water · transformation

Governs digestion, body heat and understanding. In balance: sharp, focused, warm-hearted. Out of balance: anger, acidity, skin rashes, impatience.

🌊

Kapha

Earth and water · structure

Gives the body its structure, strength and moisture. In balance: calm, steady, loving, strong. Out of balance: sluggishness, weight gain, congestion, attachment.

These ideas are how the tradition describes the body. They are not the same as modern medical diagnoses, and modern science has not confirmed doshas as measurable things. Many people still find them a helpful way to think about their habits.

Pitta seems strongest in you. Tradition suggests cooling foods, not skipping meals, avoiding too much heat and spice, and time to relax.

Kapha seems strongest in you. Tradition suggests lighter, warm food, regular exercise, waking early, and trying new things.

Ritucharya

Living with the seasons

India has six seasons; North America has four, but the idea is the same: eat and live according to the weather outside.

❄️

Winter (Hemanta, Shishira)

Digestion is strongest. Warm, nourishing, cooked food; soups, sesame, ghee and ginger tea. Oil massage for dry skin.

🌱

Spring (Vasanta)

Kapha melts like snow. Lighter food, less fried and sweet, more movement and early rising.

☀️

Summer (Grishma)

Stay cool. Buttermilk, coconut water, cucumber, sweet fruits; avoid the midday sun and heavy spice.

🍂

Rains and autumn (Varsha, Sharad)

Digestion is weaker in the rains: eat freshly cooked, warm, simple food. In autumn, as heat builds up, favour cooling, sweet and bitter tastes.

Your kitchen

The spice box as a first-aid kit

🟡 Turmeric (haldi)

Tradition says: Cleanses and heals; used in cooking, in golden milk and as a paste on small cuts.

Research says: Studied a lot, but there is little strong proof for most uses, and the body absorbs very little of it. Strong turmeric supplements have been linked to rare liver problems. Cooking amounts are fine.

🫚 Ginger (adrak, saunth)

Tradition says: Called vishvabheshaja, the universal medicine. Kindles agni and helps digestion.

Research says: Some evidence that it can ease nausea, including in pregnancy. Ask your doctor before taking it as a supplement.

🌿 Tulsi (holy basil)

Tradition says: Sacred to Vishnu; tulsi tea for coughs and colds.

Research says: A few small studies, but not enough to know if it works for any illness. As a tea it is generally safe.

🌾 Cumin, ajwain and fennel (saunf)

Tradition says: Digestive spices; a pinch of saunf after meals.

Research says: Little research, but safe in food amounts.

🧈 Ghee

Tradition says: Nourishing; carries herbs into the body; good for agni in small amounts.

Research says: It is mostly saturated fat, so use it in moderation, especially with heart or cholesterol concerns.

Stay safe

What every family should know

Ayurvedic habits like good routine, home-cooked food and spices are safe for almost everyone. Ayurvedic products bought as pills and powders are a different matter.

Heavy metals. A study in the Journal of the American Medical Association (2008) bought 193 Ayurvedic products online and found lead, mercury or arsenic in about 1 in 5 of them, both US-made and India-made. Products made by the traditional rasa shastra method, which deliberately uses metals, were more than twice as likely to contain them.

Warnings. The US Food and Drug Administration has issued warnings and import alerts about some Ayurvedic products because of lead and mercury, and health agencies in Canada, Australia and New Zealand have reported lead poisoning linked to them.

Few rules in the US. No US state licenses Ayurvedic practitioners, and Ayurvedic products are sold as supplements, which are not tested the way medicines are.

Use Ayurveda wisely

  • Use spices and herbs freely in cooking; that is the safest part of Ayurveda.
  • Before taking any Ayurvedic tablet or powder, tell your doctor, especially if you take other medicines, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or it is for a child.
  • Buy only from brands whose products are tested by an independent lab for heavy metals, and never products that list metals or "bhasma" without a doctor's advice.
  • Never use Ayurveda to delay seeing a doctor about a health problem.
