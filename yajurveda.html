In 30 seconds

  • The Veda of action: the handbook of the priest who performs the yajna (fire ritual), mostly in prose formulas.
  • It has two branches: the White (Shukla), with mantras and explanation kept apart, and the Black (Krishna), with them mixed together.
  • Beyond ritual it holds much-loved prayers: the Shanti mantra for peace everywhere, the Rudram to Shiva, and "may my mind think good thoughts."
  • Its last chapter is the Isha Upanishad: God dwells in everything, so enjoy life without greed.

The Veda of action

What is the Yajurveda?

Yajus means a sacrificial formula, usually in prose. The Yajurveda is the working manual of the adhvaryu, the priest who actually builds the altar, kindles the fire and makes the offerings, saying the right words at each step. It covers the daily fire offerings, the new-moon and full-moon rites, the soma sacrifice, the building of the great fire altar, and royal ceremonies such as the Rajasuya and Ashvamedha.

But it is not only ritual. It also contains some of the most loved prayers in Hinduism: the Rudram to Shiva, the Shanti mantra for peace in all creation, the Shiva Sankalpa prayer for a good mind, and the Isha Upanishad.

Two branches

White and Black Yajurveda

Tradition tells a story. The sage Yajnavalkya quarrelled with his teacher Vaishampayana, who asked him to give back everything he had learned. Yajnavalkya vomited out the knowledge, and the other students, taking the form of partridges (tittiri), ate it up: that became the Taittiriya, or Black, Yajurveda. Yajnavalkya then prayed to the Sun, who taught him a fresh, "white" Yajurveda.

Shukla (White) Yajurveda

Vajasaneyi Samhita · 40 chapters · about 1,975 mantras

Mantras and their explanation are kept in separate books. Two recensions: Madhyandina (most of North India) and Kanva (Maharashtra, Odisha). Its Brahmana is the huge Shatapatha Brahmana; its Upanishads are the Isha and Brihadaranyaka.

Krishna (Black) Yajurveda

Taittiriya, Maitrayani, Kathaka, Kapishthala Samhitas

Mantras and their explanations are mixed together in one text, which is why it is called "black" (mixed). The Taittiriya branch is followed widely in South India. Its Upanishads include the Taittiriya, Katha, Shvetashvatara and Maitri.

Peace everywhere (Shanti Mantra)

Shukla Yajurveda 36.17

द्यौः शान्तिरन्तरिक्षं शान्तिः पृथिवी शान्तिरापः शान्तिरोषधयः शान्तिः ।
वनस्पतयः शान्तिर्विश्वेदेवाः शान्तिर्ब्रह्म शान्तिः सर्वं शान्तिः शान्तिरेव शान्तिः सा मा शान्तिरेधि ॥

dyauḥ śāntir antarikṣaṃ śāntiḥ pṛthivī śāntir āpaḥ śāntir oṣadhayaḥ śāntiḥ | vanaspatayaḥ śāntir viśve devāḥ śāntir brahma śāntiḥ sarvaṃ śāntiḥ śāntir eva śāntiḥ sā mā śāntir edhi ||

Word by word
द्यौः
heaven
अन्तरिक्षम्
the sky, the space between
पृथिवी
earth
आपः
waters
ओषधयः
healing herbs
वनस्पतयः
trees
विश्वे देवाः
all the gods
सा मा शान्तिः एधि
may that peace be mine
Translation

May there be peace in the heavens, peace in the sky, peace on earth. Peace in the waters, peace in the healing herbs, peace in the trees. Peace in all the gods, peace in Brahman, peace in everything. Peace itself be peaceful. May that peace come to me.

In simple words

The prayer starts from the sky and comes down to the earth, water and plants, and only at the very end asks for peace for oneself. Inner peace is connected to peace in nature and society. This is recited at the end of almost every Hindu ceremony.

📖 A story for this verse

An everyday story

Every night, after the family prayer, Neel's father ended with "Om shanti, shanti, shanti."

"Why do we say shanti three times, Papa? Once isn't enough?"

His father counted on his fingers. "Our tradition says trouble comes from three places. One: from inside ourselves, like illness, worry or anger. Two: from other beings around us, like a bully at school or a mosquito at night. Three: from forces bigger than us, like storms and floods. So we ask for peace three times, one for each."

Neel thought about it. "So the first shanti is the one I can do most about."

His father laughed. "Exactly. The Shanti Mantra asks for peace in the sky, the earth, the waters and the plants. But it begins with a calm person saying it."

Live a hundred years, working

Shukla Yajurveda 40.2 (Isha Upanishad 2)

कुर्वन्नेवेह कर्माणि जिजीविषेच्छतं समाः ।
एवं त्वयि नान्यथेतोऽस्ति न कर्म लिप्यते नरे ॥

kurvann eveha karmāṇi jijīviṣec chataṃ samāḥ | evaṃ tvayi nānyatheto 'sti na karma lipyate nare ||

Word by word
कुर्वन् एव
only by doing
इह
here, in this world
कर्माणि
actions, work
जिजीविषेत्
one should wish to live
शतं समाः
a hundred years
एवं त्वयि
for you, in this way
न अन्यथा इतः अस्ति
there is no other way than this
न कर्म लिप्यते
action does not cling
नरे
to the person
Translation

Doing your work here, you should wish to live a hundred years. For you there is no other way than this. In this way, action does not bind a person.

In simple words

The Vedas do not ask us to run away from life. Live long and stay active, but work in the spirit of the first verse: knowing everything belongs to God, without greed. This is the seed of the Gita's karma yoga.

📖 A story for this verse

From the life of Maharshi Dhondo Keshav Karve

Dhondo Keshav Karve was a mathematics teacher in Pune in the late 1800s. At that time, girls were rarely sent to school, and young widows were often treated cruelly and kept from any education.

Karve gave his life to changing this. He started a home for widows, then a school for girls, and in 1916 he founded India's first university for women, now the SNDT Women's University. People mocked him and some shunned him, but he kept working, year after year, raising money a few rupees at a time.

He did not stop when he grew old. When he turned 100, the nation celebrated him, and in 1958 he received the Bharat Ratna. He died in 1962 at the age of 104, still interested in the work.

"Doing your work here, wish to live a hundred years," says the Yajurveda. Karve lived those hundred years, and they were full of work for others.

May we see a hundred autumns

Shukla Yajurveda 36.24

तच्चक्षुर्देवहितं पुरस्ताच्छुक्रमुच्चरत् ।
पश्येम शरदः शतं जीवेम शरदः शतं शृणुयाम शरदः शतं
प्र ब्रवाम शरदः शतमदीनाः स्याम शरदः शतं भूयश्च शरदः शतात् ॥

tac cakṣur devahitaṃ purastāc chukram uccarat | paśyema śaradaḥ śataṃ jīvema śaradaḥ śataṃ śṛṇuyāma śaradaḥ śataṃ pra bravāma śaradaḥ śatam adīnāḥ syāma śaradaḥ śataṃ bhūyaś ca śaradaḥ śatāt ||

Word by word
तत् चक्षुः
that eye (the Sun)
देव-हितं
dear to the gods, set by the gods
पुरस्तात्
in the east
शुक्रम्
bright, pure
उच्चरत्
rising
पश्येम
may we see
शरदः शतं
for a hundred autumns (years)
जीवेम
may we live
शृणुयाम
may we hear
प्र ब्रवाम
may we speak
अदीनाः स्याम
may we not be helpless or dependent
भूयः च शरदः शतात्
and even beyond a hundred years
Translation

That bright eye, dear to the gods, rises in the east. May we see for a hundred autumns, live for a hundred autumns, hear for a hundred autumns, speak for a hundred autumns, and live without dependence for a hundred autumns, and even more than a hundred.

In simple words

A prayer to the rising sun for a long, healthy and independent life: good eyes, good ears, a clear voice, and dignity in old age. It is often recited on birthdays.

May my mind have good intentions (Shiva Sankalpa)

Shukla Yajurveda 34.1

यज्जाग्रतो दूरमुदैति दैवं तदु सुप्तस्य तथैवैति ।
दूरङ्गमं ज्योतिषां ज्योतिरेकं तन्मे मनः शिवसङ्कल्पमस्तु ॥

yaj jāgrato dūram udaiti daivaṃ tad u suptasya tathaivaiti | dūraṅgamaṃ jyotiṣāṃ jyotir ekaṃ tan me manaḥ śivasaṅkalpam astu ||

Word by word
यत्
that which
जाग्रतः
of one who is awake
दूरम् उदैति
goes far away
दैवं
divine
तत् उ
that same
सुप्तस्य
of one who sleeps
तथा एव एति
returns in the same way
दूरंगमं
far-travelling
ज्योतिषां ज्योतिः
light of lights
एकं
the one
तत् मे मनः
may that mind of mine
शिव-संकल्पम् अस्तु
be filled with good intentions
Translation

That divine mind which travels far when we are awake, and travels the same way when we are asleep, the far-going light of all lights: may that mind of mine be filled with good intentions.

In simple words

Each of the six verses of this hymn ends with the same wish: tan me manaḥ śivasaṅkalpam astu, "may my mind think good thoughts." The mind is powerful and restless, so the first prayer is to point it towards good.

📖 A story for this verse

A traditional folk tale

A tired traveller lay down to rest under a large tree. He did not know it was a kalpavriksha, a wish-fulfilling tree.

"I'm so hungry," he thought. "If only I had a good meal." A plate of delicious food appeared in front of him. He ate happily. "Now a soft bed would be nice." A bed appeared. He lay down, amazed.

Then a worry crept in. "This is strange. What if this is a forest spirit? What if a tiger comes and eats me?" At once, a tiger came out of the bushes.

Our minds work a little like that tree. What we keep thinking about slowly shapes what we see, what we do and who we become.

That is why the Shiva Sankalpa hymn ends each verse with the same prayer: "May my mind be filled with good intentions."

Salutations to Rudra

Shukla Yajurveda 16.1 (Rudram)

नमस्ते रुद्र मन्यव उतो त इषवे नमः ।
बाहुभ्यामुत ते नमः ॥

namas te rudra manyava uto ta iṣave namaḥ | bāhubhyām uta te namaḥ ||

Word by word
नमः ते
salutation to you
रुद्र
O Rudra
मन्यवे
to your fierce power
उतो ते इषवे
and to your arrow
बाहुभ्याम्
to your two arms
उत ते नमः
and salutation to you
Translation

Salutations to your anger, O Rudra, and salutations to your arrow. Salutations also to your two arms.

In simple words

The Rudram opens by bowing even to the fierce side of God, praying that his anger and arrows turn away and become gentle (shiva). It goes on to see Rudra in everyone and everything, and contains the five-syllable mantra namaḥ śivāya. It is chanted at Shiva abhishekam.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Bhagavata Purana

The gods and the asuras agreed to churn the ocean of milk together to get the nectar of immortality. They used the mountain Mandara as the churning rod and the great serpent Vasuki as the rope.

But the first thing that rose from the ocean was not nectar. It was halahala, a terrible poison, so strong that its fumes began to burn all the worlds. The gods and asuras ran in terror. They had no answer, so they went to Shiva.

Shiva, the fierce Rudra who lives on cremation grounds and lights the fire of destruction at the end of time, felt only compassion for the frightened beings. He gathered the poison in his palm and drank it. It stayed in his throat and turned it blue; tradition adds that Parvati held his throat so it would go no further. That is why he is called Neelkanth, the blue-throated one.

The Rudra hymn salutes his anger and his arrows. But it also calls him Shiva, the auspicious one. The fiercest power in the universe is the one that swallows the poison to save the world.

The graduation address

Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11 (Krishna Yajurveda)

सत्यं वद । धर्मं चर । स्वाध्यायान्मा प्रमदः ।
…
मातृदेवो भव । पितृदेवो भव । आचार्यदेवो भव । अतिथिदेवो भव ।

satyaṃ vada | dharmaṃ cara | svādhyāyān mā pramadaḥ | … mātṛdevo bhava | pitṛdevo bhava | ācāryadevo bhava | atithidevo bhava |

Word by word
सत्यं वद
speak the truth
धर्मं चर
live by dharma
स्वाध्यायात्
from your own study
मा प्रमदः
do not neglect
मातृ-देवः भव
treat your mother as divine
पितृ-देवः भव
treat your father as divine
आचार्य-देवः भव
treat your teacher as divine
अतिथि-देवः भव
treat your guest as divine
Translation

Speak the truth. Practise dharma. Never neglect your own study. ... Treat your mother as God. Treat your father as God. Treat your teacher as God. Treat your guest as God.

In simple words

This is the advice a Vedic teacher gave students on the day they finished their studies and went home to start family life. It is still read at convocations, and "Atithi Devo Bhava" is the motto of Indian tourism.

📖 A story for this verse

From the Chandogya Upanishad (4.4)

A boy named Satyakama wanted to study with the sage Gautama. Before leaving, he asked his mother, "What is my family line? The teacher will ask."

His mother answered honestly: "I do not know, my son. When I was young, I worked as a servant in many houses, and I had you. My name is Jabala, and yours is Satyakama. Say you are Satyakama Jabala."

At the ashram the sage asked, "What is your family line?" Satyakama repeated exactly what his mother had said, hiding nothing.

The sage was moved. "Only one of true character would speak like this. Fetch the fuel, my child; I will take you as my student, for you have not left the truth." Satyakama went on to become a great teacher himself.

"Speak the truth," is the first line of the teacher's farewell advice in the Taittiriya Upanishad. Satyakama's whole life began with it.
