At a glance

From the Vedic altars to the Kerala school

Long before modern science, scholars in India measured, counted and tested. This page gathers achievements that historians and scientists accept today, from the earliest cities to 1800 CE, each with the source you can check.

Dates for ancient texts are scholars' estimates and often a range; where they disagree, we say so.

Mathematics

Geometry, numbers and infinite series

Geometry of the fire altars

Shulba Sutras, about 800–200 BCE

Fire altars had to be built to exact shapes and areas, so priests wrote manuals of rope geometry. Baudhayana's, the oldest we have, states the diagonal rule below, and the Shulba Sutras use many whole-number triples such as 5, 12, 13 and 8, 15, 17.

The diagonal rule

Baudhayana Shulba Sutra

दीर्घचतुरस्रस्याक्ष्णया रज्जुः पार्श्वमानी तिर्यङ्मानी च
यत्पृथग्भूते कुरुतस्तदुभयं करोति ॥

dīrghacaturasrasyākṣṇayā rajjuḥ pārśvamānī tiryaṅmānī ca
yat pṛthagbhūte kurutas tad ubhayaṃ karoti ||

Translation

The diagonal of a rectangle produces by itself both the areas that its two sides produce separately.

In simple words

The square on the diagonal equals the squares on the two sides added together: the rule we now call Pythagoras' theorem.

The square root of 2

Baudhayana Shulba Sutra 1.61–62

To double a square, Baudhayana needed its diagonal. His rule, 1 + 1/3 + 1/(3×4) − 1/(3×4×34) = 577/408 = 1.4142157, gives √2 correct to five decimal places.

Source: MacTutor: Baudhayana

Binary patterns and the "Meru" triangle

Pingala's Chandahshastra, about 3rd–2nd century BCE

Counting every pattern of short and long syllables in Sanskrit verse, Pingala wrote them in a form that works like binary numbers. The method leads to the meru-prastara, the triangle of numbers later known in Europe as Pascal's triangle.

Source: Pingala (citing Plofker 2009; Van Nooten 1993)

Fibonacci numbers before Fibonacci

Virahanka (600–800 CE), Gopala (before 1135), Hemachandra (about 1150)

Counting rhythms made of short and long beats, these scholars found that each count is the sum of the two before it: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13… Fibonacci wrote about the same sequence in 1202.

Source: Hemachandra and the Fibonacci numbers · Pingala (citing Plofker 2009; Van Nooten 1993)

Place value and zero

Aryabhata 499 CE; Brahmagupta 628 CE; Gwalior inscription 876 CE

Aryabhata names the places of a number, each ten times the last (verse below). Brahmagupta, in his Brahmasphutasiddhanta, set out rules for working with zero and with negative numbers, which he called "debts": a number plus or minus zero is unchanged.

On the wall of the Chaturbhuj temple at Gwalior, dated 876 CE, the numbers 270 and 50 end in a round zero: the earliest known stone inscription with the zero we still write. These numerals passed to Arab scholars such as al-Khwarizmi and al-Kindi, and reached Europe around the 12th century.

Each place is ten times the last

Aryabhatiya, Ganitapada 2

एकं च दश च शतं च सहस्रं त्वयुतनियुते तथा प्रयुतम् ।
कोट्यर्बुदं च वृन्दं स्थानात्स्थानं दशगुणं स्यात् ॥

ekaṃ ca daśa ca śataṃ ca sahasraṃ tv ayutaniyute tathā prayutam |
koṭyarbudaṃ ca vṛndaṃ sthānāt sthānaṃ daśaguṇaṃ syāt ||

Translation

One, ten, hundred, thousand, ten thousand, a hundred thousand, a million, ten million, a hundred million and a thousand million: from place to place, each is ten times the one before.

In simple words

This is the decimal place-value idea that every number we write today depends on.

Pi = 3.1416

Aryabhata's Aryabhatiya, 499 CE

Aryabhata finished the Aryabhatiya at the age of 23. In a single verse he gives the circle's circumference in a way that works out to π = 3.1416.

Pi to four decimal places

Aryabhatiya, Ganitapada 10

चतुरधिकं शतमष्टगुणं द्वाषष्टिस्तथा सहस्राणाम् ।
अयुतद्वयविष्कम्भस्यासन्नो वृत्तपरिणाहः ॥

caturadhikaṃ śatam aṣṭaguṇaṃ dvāṣaṣṭis tathā sahasrāṇām |
ayutadvayaviṣkambhasyāsanno vṛttapariṇāhaḥ ||

Translation

Add four to one hundred, multiply by eight, and add sixty-two thousand: this is the approximate circumference of a circle whose diameter is twenty thousand.

In simple words

62,832 ÷ 20,000 = 3.1416. Aryabhata even calls it āsanna, "approximate".

The chakravala method

Brahmagupta 628 CE; Jayadeva about 950–1000; Bhaskara II (1114–1185)

Indian mathematicians solved equations of the form Nx² + 1 = y² in whole numbers. Building on Brahmagupta, Jayadeva and Bhaskara II developed the cyclic "chakravala" method; Bhaskara's example 61x² + 1 = y² has the smallest answer x = 226,153,980 and y = 1,766,319,049. Fermat set the same problem as a challenge in 1657, and a general proof in Europe came only with Lagrange in 1766–68.

Source: MacTutor: Brahmagupta · Chakravala method · MacTutor: Bhaskara II

Trigonometry

Aryabhata 499 CE; Varahamihira, 6th century CE

Aryabhata made a table of sines at steps of 3¾ degrees. Varahamihira used identities such as sin²x + cos²x = 1 and made a more accurate sine table.

Source: MacTutor: Aryabhata I · MacTutor: Varahamihira

Infinite series in Kerala

Madhava of Sangamagrama, about 1350–1425; Nilakantha about 1500; Jyesthadeva about 1530

Madhava found infinite series for π, sine, cosine and the arctangent, and used them to give π = 3.14159265359, correct to 11 decimal places. Europe rediscovered these series more than 200 years later. Later Kerala scholars recorded the results: Nilakantha in the Tantrasangraha, and Jyesthadeva, with proofs, in the Malayalam Yuktibhasha.

Source: MacTutor: Madhava of Sangamagrama · Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics

Astronomy and calendar

Reading the sky

A calendar from the sky

Vedanga Jyotisha of Lagadha; its sky positions point to about 1350 BCE

This early astronomy text sets a lunar-solar calendar for the timing of rituals: a five-year cycle of 1,830 days and 62 lunar months, with extra months added to keep moon and sun in step. It places the winter and summer solstices among the stars, which is how scholars date it; the exact date is debated.

Source: Encyclopedia.com: Vedanga Jyotisha (Subhash Kak)

Aryabhata's astronomy

Aryabhatiya, 499 CE

Aryabhata taught that the daily turning of the sky is really the Earth spinning on its axis (verse below). He explained eclipses of the sun and moon by the shadows of the Earth and moon, at a time when many blamed the demon Rahu, and gave the year as 365 days, 6 hours, 12 minutes and 30 seconds.

The Earth turns, not the sky

Aryabhatiya, Golapada 9

अनुलोमगतिर्नौस्थः पश्यत्यचलं विलोमगं यद्वत् ।
अचलानि भानि तद्वत् समपश्चिमगानि लङ्कायाम् ॥

anulomagatir nausthaḥ paśyaty acalaṃ vilomagaṃ yadvat |
acalāni bhāni tadvat samapaścimagāni laṅkāyām ||

Translation

Just as a person in a boat moving forward sees the still objects on the bank moving backward, so the still stars are seen at Lanka (on the equator) moving straight to the west.

In simple words

The stars only seem to move because the Earth itself turns.

Five systems in one book

Varahamihira's Pancha-siddhantika, 6th century CE

Varahamihira summarised five earlier astronomical systems, the Surya, Romaka, Paulisa, Vasishtha and Paitamaha siddhantas. Several are otherwise lost, so his book is one of the main sources for Indian astronomy before Aryabhata.

Source: MacTutor: Varahamihira

Jantar Mantar, Jaipur

Built by Sawai Jai Singh II, early 18th century

Jai Singh II built this stone observatory to measure the positions of the sun, moon and stars with the naked eye. It has about 20 main fixed instruments, some the largest of their kind ever built, and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Source: UNESCO: The Jantar Mantar, Jaipur

Medicine and surgery

Healing by observation

Charaka Samhita: medicine by reasoning

Between about the 4th century BCE and 2nd century CE (Meulenbeld)

In 8 books and 120 chapters, it teaches diagnosis from causes and symptoms, careful examination of the patient, and diet as a basis of health. It also sets out a physician's training and duties, including truthfulness, the patient's consent and privacy, and lifelong learning.

Source: Charaka Samhita (citing Meulenbeld)

Sushruta Samhita: surgery

Begun in the last centuries BCE; oldest manuscript 878 CE

Sushruta describes many operations, including more than 15 ways to rebuild a nose, among them the first written record of a cheek-flap repair, and skin grafts. He tells students to learn anatomy by dissecting a body and to practise cutting on gourds before touching a patient.

Source: Sushruta Samhita (citing Meulenbeld, Loukas)

Inoculation against smallpox

Described in Bengal by J. Z. Holwell, 1767

Before vaccines, Brahmin inoculators in Bengal protected people by giving them a small, controlled dose of smallpox. The British surgeon Holwell watched the practice and published a detailed account of it in London in 1767.

Source: J. Z. Holwell, An Account of the Manner of Inoculating for the Small Pox in the East Indies (1767)

Language, metals and engineering

Making things that last

Panini's grammar

Ashtadhyayi, about the 6th–5th century BCE (dates vary)

About 4,000 short rules in eight chapters describe the whole of Sanskrit and set the standard for the classical language. Its analysis of sounds and word-forms was more complete than anything in the West until modern times, and linguists still study it as a model formal system.

Source: Britannica: Panini

Water engineering at Dholavira

Harappan city, about 3000–1500 BCE

In the dry Rann of Kutch, this planned city stored water from two seasonal streams in a chain of reservoirs, with storm-water drains and a drainage network. Its streets follow the cardinal directions.

Source: UNESCO: Dholavira, a Harappan city

The iron pillar that does not rust

Delhi, 5th century CE, time of Chandragupta II

This 7.2-metre pillar weighs more than six tonnes and has stood for about 1,600 years with almost no rust. Metallurgists at IIT Kanpur found why: iron rich in phosphorus, made by the smiths' own method, forms a thin protective film that has grown only about a twentieth of a millimetre in all that time.

Source: Iron pillar of Delhi (citing R. Balasubramaniam, IIT Kanpur)

Wootz steel

South India and Sri Lanka, from about the mid-1st millennium BCE

Smiths heated iron ore with carbon from bamboo and leaves in sealed clay crucibles to make a high-carbon steel, with early furnaces found at Kodumanal in Tamil Nadu. Traded westward, it became the famous "Damascus" sword steel.

Source: Wootz steel (citing Srinivasan and Ranganathan)

Distilling zinc at Zawar

Rajasthan; furnaces about 1,000 years old, large works by the 14th century

Zinc turns to vapour at the heat needed to free it from its ore, so it must be caught as vapour. At Zawar, ore was heated in clay retorts and the zinc vapour cooled downward, away from air, in furnace blocks of 252 retorts at a time. In England, William Champion patented a zinc process only in 1742.

Source: Chemistry World: How excavations in India changed our view on industrialisation

Rani-ki-Vav stepwell

Patan, Gujarat, 11th century CE

A water well built as an upside-down temple: seven levels of stairs lead down to a tank 23 metres below ground, with a well shaft about 30 metres deep. It stores water and honours its sanctity, and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Source: UNESCO: Rani-ki-Vav (the Queen's Stepwell)
